Abstract: Noise reduction methods have been proposed for various loud noises. However, in a quiet
indoor environment, even small noises often cause discomfort. One of the small noises that causes
discomfort is noise with resonant frequencies. Since resonant frequencies are often high frequencies,
it is difficult to apply conventional active noise control methods to them. To solve this problem,
we focused on auditory masking, a phenomenon in which synthesized sounds increase the audible
threshold. We have performed several studies on reducing discomfort based on auditory masking.
However, it was difficult for comfortable sound design to be achieved using the previously proposed
methods, even though they were able to reduce feelings of discomfort. Here, we focus on a pleasant
sound: music. Comfortable sound design is made possible by introducing music theory into the
design of masker signals. In this paper, we therefore propose comfortable sound design based on
auditory masking with chord progression and melody generation to match the peak frequencies of
dental treatment noises.

1. Introduction
Unpleasant noise, which often interferes with our lives, is a significant social problem.
Therefore, various methods have been studied to overcome noise problems. Passive noise
control [1,2] is one of the traditional noise control methods and involves sound insulation
using barriers or soundproofing materials. Passive noise control is used to reduce loud
noises generated by traffic and construction sites. Earplugs and earmuffs are also forms of
passive noise control. However, it is known that the noise reduction performance depends
on the thickness of soundproofing walls, and it is difficult to suppress noise that includes
low frequencies. Additionally, due to sound diffraction, its performance is significantly
degraded in indoor environments. Earplugs and earmuffs also inhibit communication
through conversation. On the other hand, active noise control [3,4] has been proposed,
which is a technique to reduce the sound pressure of noise by emitting the inverse phase
of the noise from a secondary source. Noise-canceling headphones are the most famous
example of active noise control and are extremely effective products. Active noise control is
excellent at suppressing noise in the low-frequency band, but it is difficult to suppress noise
in the high-frequency band, and like passive noise control, its performance is significantly
degraded in indoor environments.
Methods for noise suppression have been proposed in various studies, and many
products aimed at reducing noise power are on the market. However, in quiet surroundings,
we often perceive noise as unpleasant even if the noise is low-power. In particular, in the

peak frequencies include factory noise and railway brake noise. Additionally, dental
treatment noises with high-frequency noise cause strong unpleasant and fearful feelings
in many patients and doctors [6,7]. It is considered that such low-power noise problems
cannot be solved only by reducing the sound pressure level of the noise. Our research thus
focuses on reducing sound that causes unpleasant feelings not by reducing the noise power
but by adding masker signals to the noise based on auditory masking. Auditory masking
is one of the auditory properties of the human ear, and it is the phenomenon in which one
sound is drowned out by another sound. It has been studied using various methods [8–23].
In addition, research has been conducted to elucidate the mechanisms behind pleasant
sounds [24–33] and on comfortable sound design [34–37]. We have also found that there is
a trade-off between sound design for comfort and for reductions in discomfort [38].
We have proposed auditory-masking-based discomfort reduction methods for various
noisy environments, such as fan noise [39], factory noise [40], railway brake noise [41],
and dental treatment noise [42–47]. These methods designed masker signals using a bandpass filter with the critical bandwidth [48]. In methods [42,43], auditory masking that
corresponds to the peak frequencies of the noise is applied, achieving good performance
in reducing discomfort. However, these methods struggle to create pleasant sounds due
to the lack of comfort in the masker signals. In this study, we focus on music as a source
of pleasant sound. According to music theory, music consists of three elements: rhythm,
melody, and harmony [49–53]. In conventional methods [44–47], we proposed comfortable
sound design based on forming single chords for dental treatment noises. However,
the conventional methods had difficulty adapting to variations in the peak frequency of
the dental treatment noises because they only used the Canon progression as the chord
progression. Furthermore, we previously proposed comfortable sound design using only
chord progressions to mask dental treatment noises [38]. However, this method [38]
encountered challenges in improving comfort scores due to the trade-off between pleasant
sound design and effectively reducing discomfort.
In this paper, we propose comfortable sound design based on auditory masking
with the melody and harmony (chord progression) matched to the peak frequency of the
dental treatment noise. The chord progression sounds are generated using major triads
of sawtooth waves and the music theory of diatonic chords. The melody sounds are
generated using the chord progression and automatic music composition software Flow
Machines [54], developed by Sony Computer Science Laboratories. The masker sounds are
produced by combining the chord progression and the melody sounds in real time using a
pre-designed database. The proposed method can track the peak frequency fluctuations
in dental treatment sounds in automatically generating chord progressions. Additionally,
automatic composition is used for the melody to transform unpleasant dental treatment
sounds into more pleasant ones, which is a key novelty of the proposed method. By listening
to the masker sounds simultaneously with the dental treatment noise, the overall sound
is designed to be more comfortable and reduce discomfort. Finally, we confirmed the
effectiveness of the proposed method through evaluation experiments.

3. Conventional Music Theory for Comfortable Sound Design
Several music theories [49–53] are used to automatically generate chord progressions.
A chord is an element of a chord progression and a collection of multiple notes. Chord
progressions are created by concatenating chords and applying the rules of diatonic chords.
Modulation of chord progressions must be carried out according to the rules of diatonic
chords. The rules of chords are explained in Section 3.1, the rules of diatonic chords are
explained in Section 3.2, the rules of modulation are explained in Section 3.3, and the rules
of melody are explained in Section 3.4.
3.1. Music Theory of Chords [49]
In music theory, chords are a group of sounds combined according to a specific order.
There are chords that are consonant and comfortable and chords that are dissonant and
harsh. Among consonant chords, major triads and octaves are particularly comfortable.

3.3. Music Theory of Modulation [52]
Chord progressions are constructed based on a fundamental tone. Modulation is
achieved by switching the key from the fundamental tone to another key, which can add
emphasis to the musical composition. To achieve the correct modulation, common chords
are required between the current key and the subsequent key. In Table 1, the same chord
Am is found in the VI of the C key and the III of the F key. This common chord is called a
pivot chord, and it is used for smooth modulation in musical composition.
3.4. Music Theory of Melody [53]
A melody is a combination of the pitch and the rhythm, while more figuratively,
the term can include successions of other musical elements, such as tonal color. It is the
foreground to the background accompaniment. A line or part need not be a foreground
melody. Melodies often consist of one or more musical phrases or motifs and are usually
repeated throughout a composition in various forms. Melodies may also be described
by their melodic motion or the pitches or the intervals between pitches (predominantly
conjunct or disjunct or with further restrictions), pitch range, tension and release, continuity
and coherence, cadence, and shape.

7. Conclusions
In this paper, we proposed comfortable sound design based on auditory masking
using chord progressions and melodies that correspond to the peak frequencies of dental
treatment noises. From the evaluation experiments, we confirmed that the proposed
method improves both comfort scores and discomfort reduction scores. In particular,
the proposed method showed superior experimental results compared to the conventional
method for air turbines and contra-angles with significant peak frequency fluctuations. This
indicates the effectiveness of the proposed method in terms of following the peak frequency,
generating chord progressions, and automatically composing melodies. However, for a
scaler, the performance of the proposed method was comparable to that of the conventional
method, and the overall comfort rating was also lower. In the future, we intend to add
rules for the chord progressions based on musical recommendations and use instrumental
sounds for the melodies to further improve comfortable sound design.
